Every flagship AI accelerator Nvidia ships has a secret bottleneck: the math cores are so fast that they spend most of their time waiting for data to arrive. In 2013, SK Hynix built the first High Bandwidth Memory chip for a graphics card market that barely wanted it. Today, that vertical stack of silicon has triggered a global memory shortage from 2025 onward, and made one South Korean memory maker indispensable to the AI boom.

Using a simple kitchen-and-skyscraper analogy, we break down how HBM stacks thin DRAM dies vertically, why 2.5D silicon interposers and Through-Silicon Vias beat flat circuit boards, how capacity limits force massive AI models across multiple GPUs, and why SK Hynix's biggest triumph is also its sharpest vulnerability.

You'll learn:
- Why compute got thousands of times faster while memory bandwidth lagged behind.
- How SK Hynix and AMD shipped the first High Bandwidth Memory in 2013 and 2015.
- How vertical DRAM towers, TSVs, and 2.5D silicon interposers move hundreds of gigabytes per second.
- Why limited HBM capacity forces hyperscalers to split models across multi-GPU clusters.
- The Tradeoff: why custom pre-sold HBM towers lock memory makers into extreme cyclical risk.
